Overview

This notebook develops three ideas from Lecture 6:

  1. the Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) theorem and the meaning of “holding other regressors constant”;
  2. simple OLS as a special case of FWL, where partialling out the intercept is just demeaning;
  3. the decomposition of variation into TSS, ESS, and RSS, leading to \(R^2\) and adjusted \(R^2\).

The emphasis is on connecting the algebra to calculations that we can verify directly in R.

2 FWL in scalar form

Suppose we are interested in the coefficient on \(X_3\) while controlling for \(X_2\).

FWL says that we can recover the same coefficient using three regressions.

2.1 Step 1: Partial \(X_2\) out of \(X_3\)

Regress \(X_3\) on the intercept and \(X_2\):

\[ X_{3i} = \gamma_1 + \gamma_2 X_{2i} + v_i. \]

# STEP 1 OF FWL:
# Regress X3 on X2. R includes an intercept automatically, so this is
# X3_i = gamma_1 + gamma_2 X2_i + v_i.
reg_X3_on_X2 <- lm(X3 ~ X2, data = df)

# resid() extracts the OLS residuals: observed X3 minus fitted X3.
# These residuals are the part of X3 not linearly explained by X2
# (and by the intercept).
v_hat <- resid(reg_X3_on_X2)

# cbind() places objects side by side so we can inspect the original
# data together with the new residual vector.
cbind(df, v_hat)

The residual \(\widehat v_i\) is the part of \(X_{3i}\) not linearly explained by the intercept and \(X_{2i}\).

Check the OLS orthogonality conditions:

# With an intercept, OLS residuals sum to zero.
# round(..., 12) hides tiny computer-rounding errors such as 1e-15.
round(sum(v_hat), 12)
[1] 0
# OLS residuals are also orthogonal to each included regressor.
# Multiplying observation by observation and summing computes v_hat'X2.
round(sum(v_hat * df$X2), 12)
[1] 0

Both are zero up to numerical rounding.

2.2 Step 2: Partial \(X_2\) out of \(Y\)

Regress \(Y\) on the intercept and \(X_2\):

\[ Y_i = \delta_1 + \delta_2 X_{2i} + u_i. \]

# STEP 2 OF FWL:
# Regress Y on the same variable(s) that we partialled out of X3.
reg_Y_on_X2 <- lm(Y ~ X2, data = df)

# These residuals are the part of Y not linearly explained by X2
# (and by the intercept).
u_hat <- resid(reg_Y_on_X2)

cbind(df, u_hat)

Again, \(\widehat u_i\) is the part of \(Y_i\) not linearly explained by the intercept and \(X_{2i}\).

2.3 Step 3: Regress residual \(Y\) on residual \(X_3\)

Now estimate

\[ \widehat u_i = b_3 \widehat v_i + r_i. \]

There is no intercept: both residual vectors already have sample mean zero.

# STEP 3 OF FWL:
# Regress the residualized Y on the residualized X3.
# IMPORTANT R FORMULA SYNTAX:
#   u_hat ~ v_hat      includes an intercept by default.
#   u_hat ~ v_hat - 1  REMOVES the intercept.
# We remove it here because both residual vectors already have mean zero,
# so the regression line should pass through the origin.
fwl_scalar <- lm(u_hat ~ v_hat - 1)

3 The familiar covariance/variance formula after partialling out

Because Step 3 is a simple regression through the origin,

\[ \widehat\beta_3 = \frac{\widehat{\mathbf v}'\widehat{\mathbf u}} {\widehat{\mathbf v}'\widehat{\mathbf v}}. \]

Since the residuals have zero sample means, this is equivalently

\[ \widehat\beta_3 = \frac{\widehat{\operatorname{Cov}}(\widehat v,\widehat u)} {\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\widehat v)}. \]

# Compute the simple-regression slope directly from residuals.
# sum(v_hat * u_hat) is the cross-product v_hat'u_hat.
# sum(v_hat^2) is v_hat'v_hat.
beta3_crossprod <- sum(v_hat * u_hat) / sum(v_hat^2)

# Because both residual vectors have mean zero, the same slope can be
# written as sample covariance divided by sample variance.
beta3_covvar <- cov(v_hat, u_hat) / var(v_hat)

c(
  full_regression = beta3_full,
  residual_crossproducts = beta3_crossprod,
  residual_covariance_variance = beta3_covvar
)

The multiple-regression coefficient is therefore the familiar simple-OLS slope formula applied after the other included regressors have been partialled out.

5 Simple OLS as a special case of FWL

Before returning to simple OLS, it is useful to see what regression on a constant alone does.

5.1 Regression on a constant gives the sample mean

Suppose we regress \(\mathbf y\) on only a column of ones:

\[ \mathbf y = \boldsymbol\iota\beta_0 + \boldsymbol\varepsilon, \]

where \(\boldsymbol\iota\) is the \(n\times1\) vector of ones.

The OLS estimator is

\[ \widehat\beta_0 = (\boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\iota)^{-1} \boldsymbol\iota'\mathbf y. \]

Since

\[ \boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\iota=n \]

and

\[ \boldsymbol\iota'\mathbf y = \sum_{i=1}^n y_i, \]

we obtain

\[ \boxed{ \widehat\beta_0 = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n y_i = \bar y. } \]

Therefore the fitted-value vector is

\[ \widehat{\mathbf y} = \boldsymbol\iota\widehat\beta_0 = \boxed{\boldsymbol\iota\bar y}. \]

So:

\[ \boxed{\text{Regressing a variable on a constant returns its sample mean.}} \]

5.1.1 Verify numerically in R

y_const <- df$Y

# In an R regression formula, ~ 1 means "include ONLY an intercept."
# There are no varying regressors on the right-hand side.
# OLS therefore chooses the single constant that best fits all y values.
constant_only <- lm(y_const ~ 1)

# coef(constant_only)[1] extracts the estimated intercept.
# mean(y_const) computes the ordinary arithmetic sample mean.
# The two numbers should be identical.

  sample_mean = mean(y_const)
)

Because every fitted value equals \(\bar y\), the residuals are

\[ \widehat\varepsilon_i = y_i-\widehat y_i = y_i-\bar y. \]

In vector form,

\[ \boxed{ \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon} = \mathbf y-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y. } \]

So a regression on a constant does two things at once:

\[ \boxed{ \text{fitted values}=\text{sample mean}, \qquad \text{residuals}=\text{deviations from the sample mean}. } \]

Verify directly in R:

# resid() returns y_i - yhat_i.
# In this constant-only regression yhat_i = ybar for every i,
# so each residual must equal y_i - ybar: a deviation from the mean.

  y = y_const,

  residual = resid(constant_only),

This is exactly what the residual-maker for the constant does:

\[ \mathbf M_0\mathbf y = \mathbf y-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y. \]

This also shows why a regression on a constant is not indeterminate. With one column, the design matrix has full column rank because

\[ \boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\iota=n>0. \]

The problem arises only if another regressor is perfectly collinear with the constant.

5.2 From the mean to demeaning

Now consider simple regression with an intercept:

\[ y_i=\beta_1+\beta_2x_i+\varepsilon_i. \]

If the only variable being partialled out is the intercept, define \(\boldsymbol\iota\) as the \(n\times1\) vector of ones.

The projection matrix onto the intercept is

\[ \mathbf P_0 = \boldsymbol\iota (\boldsymbol\iota'\boldsymbol\iota)^{-1} \boldsymbol\iota' = \frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol\iota\boldsymbol\iota'. \]

Therefore

\[ \mathbf P_0\mathbf y = \frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol\iota\boldsymbol\iota'\mathbf y = \boldsymbol\iota\bar y. \]

The associated residual-maker is Greene’s deviation-from-the-mean matrix:

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf M_0 = \mathbf I-\frac{1}{n}\boldsymbol\iota\boldsymbol\iota'. } \]

Hence

\[ \mathbf M_0\mathbf y = \mathbf y-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y, \qquad \mathbf M_0\mathbf x = \mathbf x-\boldsymbol\iota\bar x. \]

In other words:

\[ \boxed{\text{Residualizing on the intercept is demeaning.}} \]

The chain is:

\[ \boxed{ \text{regress on a constant} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{sample mean} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{project onto the constant} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{demean with }\mathbf M_0 } \]

5.3 Verify numerically

6 From deviations around the mean to analysis of variation

Return to the fitted multiple regression

\[ \mathbf y = \mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} + \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon} = \widehat{\mathbf y} + \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}. \]

Premultiply by \(\mathbf M_0\):

\[ \mathbf M_0\mathbf y = \mathbf M_0\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta} + \mathbf M_0\widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}. \]

When an intercept is included, the residuals sum to zero:

\[ \boldsymbol\iota'\widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=0, \]

so demeaning the residuals does nothing:

\[ \mathbf M_0\widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon} = \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}. \]

Thus

\[ \boxed{ \mathbf M_0\mathbf y = \mathbf M_0\widehat{\mathbf y} + \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}. } \]

This says that the deviation of \(y\) from its mean splits into an explained component and a residual component.

7 TSS = ESS + RSS

Because OLS residuals are orthogonal to the columns of \(\mathbf X\),

\[ \mathbf X'\widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=\mathbf 0. \]

The fitted values \(\widehat{\mathbf y}=\mathbf X\widehat{\boldsymbol\beta}\) lie in \(\operatorname{Col}(\mathbf X)\). With an intercept, \(\boldsymbol\iota\bar y\) also lies in \(\operatorname{Col}(\mathbf X)\). Therefore

\[ (\widehat{\mathbf y}-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y)' \widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}=0. \]

The explained and residual components are orthogonal, so their squared lengths add:

\[ \boxed{ \|\mathbf y-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y\|^2 = \|\widehat{\mathbf y}-\boldsymbol\iota\bar y\|^2 + \|\widehat{\boldsymbol\varepsilon}\|^2. } \]

Define

\[ TSS = \sum_{i=1}^n(y_i-\bar y)^2, \]

\[ ESS = \sum_{i=1}^n(\widehat y_i-\bar y)^2, \]

and

\[ RSS = \sum_{i=1}^n\widehat\varepsilon_i^2. \]

Then

\[ \boxed{TSS=ESS+RSS.} \]

7.1 Verify numerically

Use the full multiple regression from Section 1.

# Save the observed Y values, fitted values, and residuals.
y_obs <- df$Y
y_hat <- fitted(full_model)
e_hat <- resid(full_model)

# Compute the sample mean of Y.
y_bar <- mean(y_obs)

# TSS: total squared variation of observed Y around its sample mean.
TSS <- sum((y_obs - y_bar)^2)

# ESS: squared variation of the fitted values around the SAME mean of Y.
ESS <- sum((y_hat - y_bar)^2)

# RSS: squared residuals, i.e. the variation left unexplained by the model.
RSS <- sum(e_hat^2)

anova_decomp <- data.frame(
  Quantity = c("TSS", "ESS", "RSS", "ESS + RSS"),
  Value = c(TSS, ESS, RSS, ESS + RSS)
)

knitr::kable(anova_decomp, digits = 6)

8 \(R^2\)

A natural goodness-of-fit measure is the fraction of total sample variation explained by the fitted regression:

\[ \boxed{ R^2=\frac{ESS}{TSS} =1-\frac{RSS}{TSS}. } \]

# Compute R-squared three different ways.
# 1) Explained variation divided by total variation.
R2_manual <- ESS / TSS

# 2) One minus the unexplained share of total variation.
R2_alt <- 1 - RSS / TSS

# 3) Ask R for the R-squared stored in the regression summary.
# The $ operator extracts a named component from an R object.
R2_lm <- summary(full_model)$r.squared

c(
  ESS_over_TSS = R2_manual,
  one_minus_RSS_over_TSS = R2_alt,
  lm_R_squared = R2_lm
)
          ESS_over_TSS one_minus_RSS_over_TSS           lm_R_squared 
             0.9526316              0.9526316              0.9526316

For OLS with an intercept,

\[ 0\le R^2\le 1. \]

Also,

\[ R^2=\operatorname{Corr}(y,\widehat y)^2. \]

c(
  R_squared = R2_lm,
  squared_corr_y_yhat = cor(y_obs, y_hat)^2
)

9 Why adding regressors cannot lower \(R^2\)

Compare a smaller model

\[ Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+e \]

with a larger model

\[ Y=\beta_0+\beta_1X_1+\beta_2X_2+e. \]

The larger model gives OLS another coefficient to choose. It can always reproduce the smaller model by setting \(\beta_2=0\).

Therefore the larger model minimizes RSS over a larger set of possible coefficient vectors:

\[ \boxed{ RSS_{\text{larger}} \le RSS_{\text{smaller}}. } \]

Since both models use the same dependent variable, TSS is unchanged, so

\[ \boxed{ R^2_{\text{larger}} \ge R^2_{\text{smaller}}. } \]

9.1 Verify with an irrelevant regressor

Add an arbitrary extra regressor to our tiny dataset. With only four observations the example is very small, so use a larger simulated dataset here.

# set.seed() makes the simulated random numbers reproducible:
# everyone running this code gets the same example.
set.seed(2026)

# Create 200 artificial observations.
n <- 200

# rnorm(n) draws n values from a standard normal distribution.
x1 <- rnorm(n)
x2 <- rnorm(n)

# z is generated independently and does NOT enter the equation for y below.
# In this simulated example it is therefore an irrelevant regressor.
z <- rnorm(n)

# Generate a random disturbance with standard deviation 2.

# Construct y so that only x1 and x2 systematically affect it.

# First fit the smaller model.
small_model <- lm(y ~ x1 + x2)

# Then add the irrelevant variable z.
# R includes an intercept in BOTH models automatically.
large_model <- lm(y ~ x1 + x2 + z)

fit_comparison <- data.frame(
  Model = c("Smaller", "Larger"),
  RSS = c(sum(resid(small_model)^2),
          sum(resid(large_model)^2)),

10 Adjusted \(R^2\)

Unadjusted \(R^2\) mechanically rewards larger models. Adjusted \(R^2\) accounts for the number of estimated parameters.

Using Greene’s notation, let \(K\) be the number of columns in \(\mathbf X\), including the intercept. Then

\[ \boxed{ \bar R^2 = 1- \frac{RSS/(n-K)}{TSS/(n-1)}. } \]

Equivalently,

\[ \boxed{ \bar R^2 = 1- \frac{n-1}{n-K}(1-R^2). } \]

Unlike \(R^2\), adjusted \(R^2\) can fall when a regressor is added. The additional variable must reduce RSS enough to compensate for the loss of a degree of freedom.

11 What \(R^2\) does – and does not – tell us

A high \(R^2\) does not imply:

  • that the regressors have causal effects;
  • that the model is correctly specified;
  • that every included regressor is economically or statistically important.

A low \(R^2\) does not imply:

  • that the coefficient estimates are uninteresting;
  • that the regressors are unrelated to the outcome;
  • that the model is necessarily badly specified.

\(R^2\) is a measure of in-sample fit, not a general measure of model quality.

12 Wrap-up

The main connections from this lecture are:

\[ \boxed{ \text{FWL} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{residualization} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{demeaning as a special case} \;\longrightarrow\; \text{variation decomposition} \;\longrightarrow\; R^2. } \]

In particular:

  • multiple-regression coefficients can be understood as simple-regression coefficients after partialling out the other included regressors;
  • \(\mathbf M_0\) residualizes with respect to the intercept and therefore converts variables to deviations from their sample means;
  • OLS orthogonality gives \(TSS=ESS+RSS\);
  • \(R^2\) measures the share of sample variation around the mean accounted for by the fitted regression;
  • adjusted \(R^2\) adds a degrees-of-freedom penalty for additional regressors.
